# Final-Round Reflection — Reinforcement-Learning Perspective

Over the course of the BBO capstone, my understanding of the exploration–exploitation trade-off changed considerably. In the early rounds, exploration had relatively high value because I had few observations and limited knowledge of the eight response surfaces. I therefore used Gaussian Process modelling, uncertainty and acquisition-function reasoning to investigate both promising and poorly understood regions. As the data set grew, exploitation became increasingly important because several functions developed clear local patterns. By the final rounds, I was no longer treating every part of the search space equally. Functions such as 5 and 7 showed consistent directions of improvement, so I progressively reduced the size of my query movements. In contrast, unexpected deterioration in Function 1 and irregular behaviour in Function 3 reminded me that continued exploitation can become risky when the apparent trend is unreliable.

This progression has strong similarities with the multi-armed bandit problem. Early in the project, I had uncertain estimates of the value of different regions and therefore needed to explore. Each new observation then changed my expectations about where future reward was most likely. Although I did not literally maintain Q-values for every possible query, the logic was similar to Q-learning: an observed result acted as feedback that either strengthened or weakened my expectation of the usefulness of a particular local strategy. For example, Function 7 improved from approximately 1.60 to 1.70, 1.74, 1.77 and finally 1.78, increasing my confidence in continued local exploitation. In Function 1, however, a sharp deterioration after moving too far changed my expectation and caused me to move back toward the previously successful region.

The iterative process also has similarities with AlphaGo Zero's feedback-driven improvement. Each round generated new evidence that influenced the next policy for selecting queries. My approach contained elements of both model-free and model-based learning. The actual function evaluations were inherently trial-and-error because the functions were hidden, which resembles model-free learning. However, I also used Gaussian Process surrogate models, Expected Improvement, feature diagnostics, clustering and PCA-informed reasoning to anticipate which future queries were likely to be valuable. This introduces a model-based planning component rather than relying purely on observed rewards.

In real optimisation problems, reinforcement-learning ideas could improve efficiency by making exploration adaptive rather than fixed. An optimiser could increase exploration when uncertainty is high or when observed rewards contradict the current model, while progressively exploiting stable high-reward regions as confidence improves. Bandit methods could allocate expensive experiments between competing regions, while value-based or policy-learning approaches could learn which sequence of experiments tends to produce the greatest long-term improvement.

The main lesson from the project is that optimisation is not simply about selecting the highest predicted value at every step. Effective optimisation requires learning from feedback, updating expectations, managing uncertainty and changing strategy as evidence accumulates. My final round therefore uses predominantly local exploitation while retaining small, deliberate movements where further information may still improve the final result.
